# Avian Flu Update

Coming to a shore near you

Like something out of an Alfred Hitchcock movie, a new breed of blockbuster may be hitting the U.S. this summer: the H5N1 (avian flu) virus. Arriving on the wings of migrating birds, California officials expect to see the first signs of flu arrive on the west coast as early as June. The H5N1 virus, which has so far overwhelmingly infected birds, could be brought to continental U.S. shores carried south by birds migrating from Asia via Alaska.

Though the H5N1 virus has already been transmitted through birds from Asia across Europe and down to Africa, human cases remain relatively uncommon. The presence of virus-infected birds would not necessarily mean a jump in strain to humans. To date, the virus has sickened 186 people in eight countries and killed 105. No birds have yet been found to carry the virus in the United States.

Getting closer to a cure

According to The New England Journal of Medicine, an experimental vaccine used in human testing has proved promising in terms of boosting immune responses against the H5N1 virus. Dr. John J. Treanor, from the University of Rochester Medical Center in New York, and his associates developed the vaccine from both an avian flu virus that killed a boy in Vietnam in 2004 and a lab strain commonly used as a platform for influenza vaccines.

The vaccine was administered to 451 healthy adults (between the ages of 18-64), and test results showed no serious side effects. In the majority of recipients, an immune response occurred that current data suggests could neutralize the avian flu virus. Though the super-strain needed for human-to-human transmission would differ from the one tested, the vaccine does bode well for further development of virus vaccines.

Super Sweetgum

A key new source of an ingredient needed to make the antiviral agent responsible for fighting bird flu has been discovered in an unlikely candidate: the Sweetgum tree. The Sweetgum’s mace-like green balls that litter the streets and sidewalks across the US contain significant amounts of shikimic acid – a starting material used to make Tamiflu.

The findings, revealed by the American Chemical Society, could help slow the spread of the virus while researchers continue to develop a true, bird flu vaccine. The Tamiflu drug, which blocks replication of the illness, is currently being stockpiled to fight a possible pandemic. Before this, shikimic acid was most commonly found in the Chinese star anise -- a fruit whose supply has dwindled as of late. The Sweetgum tree grows widely throughout the United States and other parts of the world, with each tree holding thousands of seedpods.

Want fries with that flu?

You can have them hold the honey mustard on your next grilled-chicken sandwich, but what about holding the H5N1? Don't worry; the National Chicken Council, a trade association for the poultry industry, recently developed a new program that tests chicken flocks for two dangerous strains of avian influenza -- H5N1 and H7N1 -- before they're plucked at a processing plant. So far, 93 percent of U.S. poultry companies have enrolled in the screening program.

I'll need some ID for that flu

We won’t stand a chance against a pandemic without a faster way to identify H5N1 cases — it takes 4 days right now. The most promising shortcut comes from the University of Colorado, where researchers are testing slides speckled with the DNA of various flu strains, including H5N1. When a sample of infected mucus is applied to the slide, any flu-virus RNA present will bind to its specific DNA match, producing a diagnosis in under 12 hours. The inventors hope the test will be in use at research hospitals within the year.

What to look for in a flu

Mistaking a case of avian flu for the conventional kind could end up killing you. Luckily, a new World Health Organization study reveals variations that may help distinguish between the two. Experts reviewed data from 40 people who had H5N1, and found that most suffered flu-induced runs, stomach cramps, and bleeding from the nose and gums — none of which occur with seasonal flu. If you have any of these symptoms along with a fever or shortness of breath, ask your doctor for a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test to ID the virus.

Vaccines: short on supply, high in demand

If an avian flu (H5N1) pandemic erupts in Asia, the law of supply and demand will take on a new, deadlier meaning when we deplete our small stockpile of vaccine. But Purdue researchers may have found a fix: By employing a new version of the H5N1 vaccine -- one that uses the common cold virus as a delivery method--the scientists can carry out the incubation process inside tissue cultures instead of chicken eggs, reducing production time by 5 months. Human tests of the vaccine are being planned.
