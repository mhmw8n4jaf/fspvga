# A Tale of 3 Hearts

[from developing heart](https://memorycircuits.hbsnzj.com/)

Did you know that open-heart surgery has a stink all its own? It's a burning-blood fragrance—a whiff of baking roadkill you'd swerve to avoid during a bike ride. Only deeper, richer—tinged with death, and sweetness. I know because I inhaled a lifetime's worth of open-heart fumes recently, standing at the elbow of Toby Cosgrove, M.D., chairman of cardiovascular surgery at the Cleveland Clinic. He was performing a valve repair and coronary bypass on an old high-school pal of his, and he had invited me, a heart patient at his clinic, to watch. And, surprisingly, sniff. Let me remind you: Mortality stinks.

Every channel flipper has done it: You're happily sampling the emptiness, click by click, when you flash into a gory moment on the Learning Channel. That was my life last summer: from Wild On! to a close-up of bloody surgical gloves in an instant—only for real, with my own heart in the spotlight and no remote to press. And I have to watch this program to its conclusion.

Here's the deal: I'm 5'10'', 150 pounds, 46 years old. I've been wearing 32-inch-waist jeans since college. I've had blood work done every year for the past 7, just for the sheer pleasure of acing an exam: My LDL (bad) cholesterol numbers averaged in the low 140s, my triglycerides below 160. And, until recently, those were considered Get Out of Heart Disease Free scores.

The R.N. who evaluated my last blood test wrote, "Your chemistry results look great. CAD [coronary-artery-disease] risk is under the normal range. No recommendations at this time." She confirmed what I saw in the mirror: a guy who was doing everything right. I work out at least four times a week, lifting weights, running, playing flat-out full-court basketball for an hour at a time. I've completed two marathons in the past 5 years, and pulled myself to the top of Grand Teton. I'm also an editor at the magazine you hold in your hands, so I've learned and lived as much health advice as anybody. But none of that prevented a 99 percent blockage in my heart's left anterior descending artery. And if that can happen to me, friends, it most assuredly can happen to you. Who, you?

Well, only if you're an adult American male. (Men over 45 are the most likely to be incubating the clot that will kill them.) Fifty-one percent of you guys—and that makes 60 million of you strolling around, averting your eyes from articles like this one—will discover your heart problem by having what the cardiologists chillingly call an "event." That is, a heart attack.

For an estimated 80,000 of these men, the first symptom is one not even I would miss: death. It's pure luck that I wasn't one of those surprise stiffs, with a starring role in our feature "Death by Exercise," which you'll find later in this issue. If you consider my sorry case an indictment of everything this magazine preaches, you can stop reading here. To me, it says something else: If a guy with all the right numbers, the right exercise habits, and a live-forever diet—or so I thought—is at grave risk of sudden death, then a lot of us are. It also tells me that the long line of nurses and doctors who told me I was fine, just fine, had no clue. And that should make you wonder what might be happening in your arteries right now, as well. Join me, and we'll take a look.

[Moore relates how](https://tiancansz.com/ra6yei6/)

Last summer I was building mileage for a 100-mile bicycle race, the kind of pee-my-bike-shorts challenge that forces me into ever longer, harder rides. The motivational stick was working: On the last Saturday in June, I rode for 6 hours and 60 miles through the hills in this part of Pennsylvania.

The following Sunday, on vacation on Martha's Vineyard, I wanted to run up some more mileage, so I took off for a coastal bike path to hammer the pedals. I was used to covering great distances with no complaints from my chassis. But now my engine light flickered on. Every time I accelerated, I felt a band of rasping pain across my chest and an ache in my shoulders and upper arms. I actually had to let some jokers I'd passed overtake me. I was lightheaded, embarrassed, weak. I started blaming things: the salt air, the early-morning hour, the headwind. I cranked it up again two or three times, but couldn't sustain the effort. So I stopped, exhausted, only a couple of miles into the 30 I'd planned to ride that morning. In this moment of surrender, I nervously recalled a Men's Health article we'd sent to the printer just before my vacation, in which a guy described how his heart muscle was dying while he smoked a cigar, drank a little scotch. I'd thought, You stupid bastard. But straddling my bike on that roadside, I felt just what he did: too scared to act on what I feared might be happening inside me. I turned my bike around, pale, shaky, defeated. I thought, Maybe I'll see a doctor—when I get back home. Stupid bastard.

My mind and body may have been on vacation last July, but I was dangerously active on the arterial level. Or so I learned a few months later, when I talked to P.K. Shah, M.D., director of cardiology at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles.

How could I ride for 6 hours without pain one weekend, I asked him, and nearly collapse after 10 minutes the next? He gave a knowing chuckle and patiently described a process known as "remodeling." It's even scarier than the kind your wife does. The process often starts at the branch points of certain blood vessels in your heart, your brain, your legs. If you inherit a tendency toward vulnerable blood vessels—thanks Mom, Dad—the rushing blood causes fissures to form in the lumen, the lining of your arteries. These damaged areas allow junk from your bloodstream to drop out of the flow and gather within your arterial walls. I've seen it a hundred times while shooting river rapids: The flotsam collects where the stream eddies. In your blood vessels, the most dangerous kind of detritus is called plaque, a killer mix of calcium, fat molecules, white blood cells, and scar tissue, all topped off with a fibrous protein cap that holds it in place.

Researchers estimate that half of the GIs who died in Vietnam—average age 22—already had plaque buried in their artery walls. Those dead GIs provide snapshots of what's going on in half of us. For them, the North Vietnamese intervened before heart disease could. For you, the years passed. You discovered barbecued ribs. They were tasty. You ordered them again. You ordered them for several decades. You licked your fingers and told yourself that a little weight gain is natural, that Uncle Herbie's early heart attack was a fluke, that there's no point living if you can't have the fries with that.

[healthy habits couldn't](https://metabolic.peppysun.com/tag/c-peptide-number-of-amino-acids/)

All this time, much of the fat in your diet was being picked up in your liver, turned into cholesterol, and ferried throughout your body, to help with cell repair and other useful processes.

Low-density lipoproteins are the boats cholesterol rides on, and if there are more of them than what's strictly necessary for bodily functioning, they become the flotsam in the blood-vessel eddies. As this wreckage was deposited beneath artery walls, it was joined by calcium, dead cells, scar tissue. You had your first plaque buildup. The tugboats that were supposed to clear that flotsam—the high-density lipoproteins (HDL)—might have been in short supply, so the junk accumulated. But you didn't feel a thing, because the plaque buildup caused the outer wall of the artery to expand, not the inner one. Your bloodflow wasn't interrupted one bit. Until it is, maybe catastrophically. Because of causes that aren't entirely understood—maybe a spike in blood pressure, maybe chronic inflammation—the fibrous cap holding back the plaque suddenly breaks. Or, as in my case, the cap remains intact, but a section of the nearby arterial wall erodes through.

Either way, the result is the same: Plaque is released into your bloodstream. If you're lucky, white blood cells clear it as they would clear any foreign invader. If not, a clot may blossom, cutting off the flow of blood to your heart muscle, which shuts down in response. And suddenly your coworkers are asking, "Hey, why is Andy hunched over his desk?" Your little girl is calling out, "Daddy? Daddy?" The guy at the car wash wants to know, "Hey, buddy, you want the Wheelbrite or not? Buddy?" But that didn't happen to me. No doctor can tell me why, except that maybe I'm not a clotting kind of guy. And that's how I went from Lance Heartstrong to the remodeling poster boy.

If you live through a near heart catastrophe, you tend to retrace your recent history and think about all the times when you might have spectacularly bought it. Like when I turned the final corner into Central Park during the 1999 New York City Marathon, and the crowd roar lifted me into endorphin ecstasy. Or in June of 2001, when a Grand Teton guide left me quite alone on a cliff top just below the summit, to initiate my own 200-foot rappel down to God-knows-where. Or any number of times on Valentine's weekend last year, when my wife and I pulled a John and Yoko in a hotel just off Amsterdam Avenue in New York City. Or, perhaps, on one of the many solo runs I've taken in early morning, to prepare for all of the above. But taking that kind of fall would have been considered strikingly unoriginal in my family. Because that's how my father died.

It happened at about 7:30 in the morning, December 20, 1996. The season's first real cold snap had moved in from Canada the night before, but that wasn't enough to stop my dad from going out as usual. But it may have been enough to stop his heart: A 1999 study of 250,000 men showed that a drop of 18 degrees on the thermometer can boost heart attacks and deaths from coronary artery disease by 13 percent. Richard G. Moore Jr. caught the fitness bug before the virus was even identified. Three decades ago, he was wearing canvas sneakers and Bermuda shorts and running around the block, making dogs bark and neighbors wonder. Running 3 to 5 days a week, he pushed himself until he completed the Marine Corps Marathon at age 63. And then he kept going. His hero was Jim Fixx, the running guru who dropped dead next to Route 15 in Vermont, at age 52. In later years, my dad, like his hero, took falls when he ran—breaking his wrist once, scraping up his face, shins, forearms other times—and my mother asked him to quit running. But his forward momentum was just too strong.

He finally crossed the finish line on a Friday morning. A neighbor near his Connecticut home happened to be looking out her window and saw him stagger on the running path, lower himself to the ground, grow still. She called 911. Before my mom even expected my father's return, two policemen were at her door. By the time I arrived 3 hours later, my mother had already identified the body, and my dad was utterly gone. He was 73—the average age of death for a man, with nothing average about it.

Exactly 1 month before, we had run together on that same path. It was now his custom to walk the steepest parts, and I asked him why. He told me a story. "I was out here running one morning," he said, "and I got to feeling dizzy. So I stopped." He gestured at the ground next to the path. "I lay down right there and fainted. When I came to, I realized that that's the way I wanted to die. In effect, I'd already been through it, and it wasn't that bad. I just wouldn't wake up." When I protested, he shrugged it off and began talking about an M. Scott Peck book he had just read, about heaven. It was as if death had become a new interest, something he planned to try one day. But he went about 20 years before anyone imagined he would, and in a way that gave his health-editor son the "duh!" moment of a lifetime. I never insisted that he have the cardiovascular workup that might have extended his life.

[him from developing](https://satietybrief.china-akan.com/blog/2058685719.html)

This seems a good moment to shift the focus of blame from me to a more comfortable target: the history of heart treatment, which has only recently stopped being a catch-at-the-wall affair.

[couldn't protect](https://bioresearch.wiki/gt8kz4/)

Here's my simplified version of how it has gone: Phase 1 [prehistory to 1939]: Hey, that guy is dead! What killed him? Phase 2 [1940 to 1950]: Hey, that guy is having a heart attack! Phase 3 [1951 to 2001]: Hey, that guy could have a heart attack at any minute! Let's crack him open and see if we can save him, now that he has advanced heart disease!

All along the way, there have been astonishing developments in understanding the disease, and marvelous progress in treating the symptoms that arrive after guys' arteries resemble a prizewinner at Cannoli Fest. And that's what disturbs me. This condition begins in the womb and continues, symptom-free, for four decades, until it rears up and kills the men it's been stalking.

Cardiologists are just now developing the means to deal with those 40 silent-but-deadly years, before the disease snuffs the birthday candles for men like my dad. And you. And, most important to me, me. It's never easy to return from vacation. Duly stressed, I had pulled on my chest-band of pain as I walked into the office my first day back. I downloaded the crises from my boss; then he asked me how my vacation was. I dropped a reference to chest pain. He looked up and said, "Get out of here and don't come back till you've had this thing checked out." That was an order.

Like the kid with the stolen answer key, I strode into the Heart Care Group at Lehigh Valley Hospital for a thallium stress test on the morning of July 31, 2002.

I was there to kick ass on the treadmill, show off my stamina for the cute nurses, and condescend to the old people shuffling around the halls. Oh yeah, and have a radioactive isotope light up my heart arteries. But when I mounted the treadmill, I had only about 10 minutes left of my illusory life as a healthy person. In fact, there was a cute nurse there. Her name was Kate, and we traded macho bicycling stories during my treadmill test. After my CT scan, she quietly asked me to come with her into an empty examining room. She handed me a prescription and started in on the details about how I was to take nitroglycerin—a powerful blood-vessel dilator—if I ever felt chest pressure again. If there's still pain after you take the pill, she told me, call 911. "The imaging showed a dark place on your heart—where the bloodflow isn't reaching," she told me. I was in a dark place, all right. But let me acknowledge here that what I went through was really no big deal. Happens all the time.

Thirty-four thousand men were treated for chest pain in 2000, in fact. Which is exactly the problem. A little later, a doctor described my condition—99 percent blockage of my left anterior descending artery, at the top of my heart (where it could do the most damage)—and my options: balloon angioplasty to clear the obstruction and a stent to keep the artery open, or a coronary bypass if I had multiple, or especially dangerous, blockages.

Back at the Men's Health offices my colleagues were having their first meeting to plan National Men's Health Week, year 2003. The plan: Tackle heart disease. Somebody asked, "Hey, where's Peter?" Our office manager called over to the hospital to inquire. She told the group, "They've admitted him."

They wheeled me down to the cardiac ward, where a nurse shaved my groin (there are side benefits to everything). Minutes later I was in the catheterization lab, flat on my back, wearing only a clammy antiseptic towel.

Robert Oriel, M.D., and his nurses maintained lively banter while they punctured my right femoral artery and threaded a tube up into my aortic valve and over to the trouble spot. They shot my heart with dye, and Dr. Oriel invited me to look at the gripping reality show on a TV monitor. Survivor, indeed.

[disease and what](https://cellular.wiki/0v9zys1kw/)

I could see my heart—a ghostly sack etched with dark lines—pulsating in black and white. And there it was, the pinch point of my life. God knows how enough blood was flowing to keep me alive. Perhaps exercise had encouraged other blood vessels to compensate for the blockage—a DIY bypass, as it were. Or maybe it was the clot-busting baby aspirin I'd begun taking based on advice from an article in this magazine. But nobody knows for sure. My heart was clean otherwise, so the rib-cracking option was out, for the time being. I felt nothing as they pushed aside the blockage with an inflatable balloon (I hope it said "Get well soon" on it), then slid a stent in place through the same catheter. They'd snagged the ball on the warning track; a harmless catch, not a walk-off home run. Since then, I've struck up relationships with a lot of other cool heart docs. In fact, I'm a regular slut now when it comes to cardiologists, and I've got the prescription list to prove it: Lipitor (to battle cholesterol), Plavix (to fight clotting). None of these heart men are particularly surprised that a physical specimen like me, of all people, would come down with a blocked artery. At least one doctor was kind of hostile about it. "It's like going into a health-food store and thinking you'll live forever," Barry Maron, M.D., of the Minneapolis Heart Institute Foundation, told me. "People want to believe they have control over their diseases—this silly idea that if I exercise, that's all I need to do. Coronary disease is complicated. If you have a powerful genetic factor, you could run around the world and it wouldn't matter."

Eric Topol, M.D., the chairman of the department of cardiovascular medicine at the Cleveland Clinic, displayed a gentler bedside manner. He started renovating my arteries with drug therapy, in the hope that future remodeling might be avoided.

"The myth of yesteryear was that arteries closed down slowly," said Dr. Topol. "If you have a crack in the artery wall from inflammation, you can go from a 20 percent narrowing to a 90 percent narrowing in a minute." I also took a fantastic voyage through my troubled organ, courtesy of Inner Imaging, a heart-scan clinic affiliated with Beth Israel Hospital in New York City.

"You had more calcified plaque than maybe 80 or 90 percent of people your age," said Harvey Hecht, M.D., Inner Imaging's crusading cardiologist. On the computer screen, he flipped through the electron beam tomography (EBT) scans of my heart, dealing them like tarot cards. "There are 600,000 first heart attacks every year, and 150,000 of them present as sudden death. With the EBT you could identify the pool of patients out of which 95 percent of those would emerge." Everybody out of the pool! But Dr. Hecht wasn't finished with me yet. He also played the cholesterol card.

On Lipitor, my LDL has plummeted to 57, my triglycerides to a skinny 73. And that would be sweet, if that could end my continuing risk of coronary clogging. But it can't. The numbers game works differently for every guy who plays it. Each of us has our own way of processing blood fats. What may be a trifle for Winston Churchill and Colonel Sanders can be dangerously high for Jim Fixx, my dad, and me. So Dr. Hecht adds another pill to my daily ritual: Niaspan, a prescription B-vitamin complex that should raise my HDL and mitigate the effects of the predatory lipids prowling my arteries, looking for an opening.

Maybe you look at me with my handful of pills and my kibitzing cardiologists and think, Stop him; he's out of control. Maybe you don't want to make room in your life for Lipitor, and Plavix, and Niaspan, and omega-3 fatty-acid supplements, and psyllium fiber (an excellent blood—and bowel—scrubber), as I did. But then, you haven't seen your future opened up in front of you on the cutting table at the Cleveland Clinic.

For me, the image of heart surgery is like a paranoid's screen saver, flipping back to my biggest fear when my brain is otherwise blank. I remember the tar-dappled lungs of the former smoker, the metal clamp spreading his chest cavity, the whiff of viscera and dried blood, the fat-swathed heart itself, a bit deflated when the heart-lung machine took over its duties. But I was also painfully aware of something perhaps nobody else in the operating room even considered. They'd constructed a box over the patient's head to use as a kind of staging platform for instruments. It was as if the guy wasn't there. But I looked into the box. I saw the gray, inert face of a man whose heart troubles were such that three surgeons, seven technicians, and a machine the size of a jukebox were required to shore up a leaky valve and bypass a clogged artery.

That's the face of heart disease, seven decades into its silent progression. The guy was out of it. He had yielded control of his most vital life processes to a machine, to the doctors who held his fragile life in their bloody hands. When the man's valve was repaired, Dr. Cosgrove squirted a little blood onto it and bade me lean over and take a look. It was holding fine—just like the drain in your bathtub. And I could see the bypass artery stitched in place, crawling across the surface of the guy's heart like a crimson worm. The operation was a success. But that means it came after a lifetime of failure. Dr. Cosgrove spoke quietly to one of the nurses, suggesting, "Why don't you call his family and let them know that it went well." And I thought of my own family, and how they might one day receive such a call—or a more painful one—if I don't do everything I can to stop this disease. It makes a handful of prevention pretty easy to swallow.
