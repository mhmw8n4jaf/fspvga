Explanation

Low libido in women is common and can affect quality of life and relationships. While occasional dips in desire are normal, persistent low sex drive may signal underlying hormonal or medical issues. This article breaks down the main causes doctors evaluate, explains what tests they may order, and offers guidance on next steps.

Understanding Low Libido in Women

Low libido in women refers to a reduced interest in sexual activity. It can show up as:

Little or no sexual thoughts or fantasies

Lack of desire for physical intimacy

Reduced arousal even when interested

A temporary drop is often linked to stress or fatigue. But if it lasts months and affects relationships or self-esteem, consider medical causes.

Hormonal Causes

Hormones play a key role in sexual desire. Doctors often check:

1. Estrogen Deficiency

Menopause: Natural decline in estrogen can thin vaginal tissues, causing discomfort and lower desire.

Surgical menopause: Removal of ovaries leads to abrupt estrogen loss.

Premature ovarian insufficiency: Ovaries stop producing estrogen before age 40.

2. Low Testosterone

Small amounts of testosterone are made in female ovaries and adrenal glands.

3. Thyroid Disorders

Hypothyroidism (low thyroid hormone) often leads to fatigue, weight gain, dry skin and reduced libido.

Hyperthyroidism (high thyroid hormone) can cause anxiety and irregular periods, also impacting desire.

4. High Prolactin

Prolactin, the "milk-production" hormone, normally rises during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Disrupt menstrual cycles

Decrease libido

5. Adrenal Hormone Imbalance

The adrenal glands produce DHEA and cortisol.

High cortisol (from chronic stress) can suppress sex hormones.

Low DHEA may contribute to reduced sexual desire.

Medical Conditions and Medications

Several health issues and treatments can blunt sexual desire.

1. Chronic Illness

Diabetes: Nerve damage and poor blood flow can reduce sensation and arousal.

Heart disease: Fear of cardiac events may lower desire; some heart medications can also contribute.

Kidney or liver disease: Toxin build-up and fatigue impact hormones and stamina.

2. Neurological Disorders

Conditions like multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease or spinal cord injuries can interfere with nerve signals involved in arousal and orgasm.

3. Painful Intercourse (Dyspareunia)

Endometriosis and pelvic inflammatory disease cause pelvic pain.

Vulvodynia leads to burning or stinging at the vaginal opening.

Chronic pain almost always reduces sexual desire.

4. Mental Health Conditions

Depression and anxiety often sap energy, interest and pleasure in activities, including sex.

Some antidepressants (especially SSRIs) and anti-anxiety medications can lower libido as a side effect.

What Doctors Check

When you mention persistent low libido in women, a doctor will usually:

Take a full medical and sexual history

Review medications and supplements

Ask about:

Stress levels and sleep quality

Relationship satisfaction

Frequency of sexual activity and any pain

Perform a physical exam, focusing on:

Pelvic exam

Thyroid gland

Signs of hormonal imbalance (e.g., hair distribution, skin changes)

Order laboratory tests:

Hormone panels: estrogen, testosterone, progesterone, prolactin

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)

Fasting glucose and A1C for diabetes screening

Other tests as needed (e.g., cortisol, DHEA)

Lifestyle and Relationship Factors

While hormones and health conditions are critical, don't overlook:

Stress and Fatigue: High cortisol levels and exhaustion reduce interest.

Sleep Quality: Poor sleep can lower testosterone and increase irritability.

Body Image: Feeling self-conscious may block desire.

Emotional Intimacy: Communication, trust and emotional connection fuel sexual desire.

Simple steps often help:

Prioritize 7–9 hours of sleep nightly

Practice stress-management (meditation, exercise, hobbies)

Schedule date nights and open conversations about needs

When to Seek Immediate Help

While low libido itself is not usually life-threatening, certain symptoms alongside it warrant prompt medical attention:

Sudden, severe pelvic or abdominal pain

Heavy vaginal bleeding or spotting

Signs of infection (fever, unusual discharge, burning)

Mood changes with suicidal thoughts

Rapid unexplained weight changes

If you experience these, please speak to a doctor or visit the nearest emergency department.
