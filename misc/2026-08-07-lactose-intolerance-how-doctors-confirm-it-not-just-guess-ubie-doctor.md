Explanation

Lactose intolerance is common but often misunderstood. People may chalk up digestive discomfort—bloating, gas or diarrhea—to "something they ate" without realizing lactose in milk and dairy products could be the culprit. Doctors don't just guess when confirming lactose intolerance; they rely on a combination of medical history, physical exam and specific tests. This guide explains how physicians arrive at a clear diagnosis—and what you can do next.

What Is Lactose Intolerance?

Lactose is the sugar found in milk and dairy. To digest it, your body needs an enzyme called lactase, produced in the small intestine. If you make too little lactase, undigested lactose travels into the colon, where bacteria ferment it. That fermentation leads to the classic symptoms of lactose intolerance.

Common signs include:

Abdominal bloating or cramps

Excess gas

Diarrhea, often watery

Nausea (sometimes leading to vomiting)

These symptoms typically appear 30 minutes to 2 hours after eating or drinking dairy.

Why Accurate Diagnosis Matters

Self-diagnosis or trial-and-error diets can lead to:

Unnecessary restriction of calcium and vitamin D, increasing fracture risk

Misattributing symptoms to lactose when another condition (like irritable bowel syndrome or celiac disease) is to blame

Ongoing discomfort if lactose intolerance is real but unaddressed

How Doctors Confirm Lactose Intolerance

Physicians follow a stepwise approach:

Detailed Medical History and Symptom Diary

Record what you eat, when symptoms occur and their severity

Note family history: lactose intolerance often runs in families

Describe any weight loss, blood in stool or systemic symptoms (fever, fatigue) that might suggest other disorders

Physical Examination

Check for abdominal tenderness or distention

Rule out signs of other gastrointestinal diseases

Diagnostic TestsWhile an initial trial of lactose avoidance can be informative, doctors rely on objective tests when needed.

A. Lactose Hydrogen Breath Test

You ingest a measured dose of lactose.

Over the next few hours, breath samples are collected to measure hydrogen.

Elevated hydrogen indicates undigested lactose fermenting in the colon.

Advantages: high accuracy, noninvasive.

Preparation: fasting for 8–12 hours, avoid smoking and exercise before test.

B. Lactose Tolerance (Blood) Test

You drink lactose solution; blood is drawn before and at intervals afterward.

If blood glucose rises less than 20 mg/dL, your body isn't properly breaking down lactose into absorbable sugars.

Less commonly used now due to discomfort from repeated blood draws.

C. Stool Acidity Test (Primarily for Infants and Young Children)

Undigested lactose ferments and produces lactic acid.

Useful when breath or blood tests aren't practical.

D. Genetic Testing

Identifies variations in the LCT gene linked to adult-type lactose intolerance.

Not routinely needed but can confirm a lifelong predisposition.

Elimination and Challenge Diet

Under guidance, you eliminate lactose for 2–4 weeks.

Symptoms should improve if lactose is the trigger.

A supervised lactose challenge helps confirm: small amounts of dairy are reintroduced while monitoring symptoms.

Differential Diagnosis: What Else Could It Be?

Similar symptoms can arise from:

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

Fructose malabsorption

Celiac disease

Inflammatory bowel disease

Food allergies (e.g., milk protein allergy)

Accurate testing rules out these conditions and prevents unnecessary dietary restrictions.

When to Seek Further Medical Advice

Although lactose intolerance itself isn't life threatening, certain "red flag" signs warrant prompt attention:

Severe, persistent abdominal pain

Sudden, unexplained weight loss

Blood in stool or vomit

High fever or signs of infection

If you experience any of these, speak to a doctor right away.

Key Takeaways

Lactose intolerance stems from low lactase enzyme levels, leading to gas, bloating and diarrhea after dairy.

Doctors use breath tests, blood tests, stool acidity tests and genetic tests—along with dietary trials—to confirm the diagnosis.

Self-testing through dairy elimination can help, but a formal evaluation ensures you don't miss other conditions.

Treatment includes lactose-free diets, enzyme supplements and alternative calcium sources.
