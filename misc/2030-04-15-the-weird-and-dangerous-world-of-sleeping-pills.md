# The Weird and Dangerous World of Sleeping Pills

Last winter, Tiger Woods and his harem of honeys titillated the nation with so many sordid stories that the oddest of them all was nearly overlooked: Woods's rumored recreational use of the prescription sleep medication Ambien. You could almost hear the collective "Huh?" as people tried to imagine how a widely prescribed (and seemingly benign) insomnia drug could produce an aphrodisiacal haze ideal for crazy sex romps.

Of course, Woods isn't the only celebrity whose pill-popping has publicized Ambien in ways its designers never intended. In 2006, U.S. Rep. Patrick Kennedy blamed his disorientation on the drug after he smashed his Mustang into a security barrier near the Capitol building. Lindsay Lohan cites Ambien as the trigger for her first stint in rehab--this after she took it, fell asleep on the floor next to a hotel bathtub, and woke only when the water overflowed.

Ambien (generic name zolpidem tartrate) was the first of a revolutionary class of sleeping pills that today includes Sonata (zaleplon), Lunesta (eszopiclone), and Ambien CR (an extended-release formulation). They've been nicknamed "Z drugs"--partly because of their ability to induce z's, and partly because of the ubiquity of z's in their generic names.

To marketers and many sleep researchers, these drugs deserve the hype. Boosters maintain that they're a quantum leap forward in the medical treatment of insomnia--potent, yet with fewer side effects even after people take them for a long time.

But to a growing cadre of critics, the once-bright halo over Z drugs is quickly corroding. On March 6, 2006, New York attorney Susan Chana Lask filed a class-action lawsuit against Ambien's maker, the French pharmaceutical giant Sanofi-aventis. The complaint alleged that more than 1,000 people suffered injury or damage as a direct result of their Ambien use, and included charges that the company failed to "adequately and sufficiently" warn doctors, patients, and the public about the drug's side effects. It also detailed nightmare stories of people sleep-driving, gorging themselves on food, and even waking up in jail with no memory of what had happened. Lask coined a name for these people: Ambien zombies.

The lawsuit was withdrawn a little over a year later after the FDA requested new warnings about the potential for what it called "complex sleep-related behaviors." The agency also called for a warning about another potentially lethal side effect: anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that could swell the tongue and throat, obstructing the user's airway.

But now, after 15 years of on-and-off use, I'd be lying if I said I don't hate it, too.

Shamans have given sleeping potions to people as far back as we have recorded history," says Daniel F. Kripke, M.D., a veteran sleep researcher at the University of California at San Diego. "It's a very, very old and primitive idea."

With the advent of modern pharmaceutical science, the "drowsy syrups" of antiquity have enjoyed steady improvements in efficacy, if not always safety. By the beginning of the 20th century, for instance, the sleepless had the choice of two drugs that remain in use today: chloral hydrate, the ingredient in the underworld's infamous "Mickey Finn"; and barbiturates, potent stupefiers allegedly named by their German creator after either St. Barbara or a barmaid named Barbara. Veterinary clinics still use these drugs to put animals "to sleep."

By the 1970s, a new class of drugs, the benzodiazepines, promised to replace these sledgehammers with fly swatters. Valium, Librium, and more recent "benzo" cousins like Xanax and Halcion activate gamma amino butyric acid (GABA), your brain's chief "calm down" neurotransmitter. GABA receptors come in many subtypes, and benzos bind to at least five of them. For legions of stressed-out patients, the net effect of these new drugs seemed glorious. The pills calmed jangled nerves, relaxed tense muscles, and let people drift off to peaceful slumber. Doctors were just as heartened. Unlike barbiturate overdoses, which dispatched more than a few patients to the Big Sleep, overdoses of benzodiazepines were less like likely to be fatal.

From 1969 to 1982, Valium was the most prescribed drug in the United States. In 1978 alone, Americans took 2.3 billion tablets. But even then questions about the true safety of this miracle drug were swirling. Many patients were finding that they needed to take escalating doses to fall asleep. Those who tried to quit sometimes faced rebound insomnia that was much nastier than their original sleeplessness had been. Researchers gradually came to a sobering realization: Benzodiazepines, when taken long term, could be physically addictive.

Despite the dangers of Valium-type pills, there would be no new conk-out options until 1992, when Ambien won FDA approval. Its cousin drug, Sonata, followed in 1999, and then Lunesta received the all-clear in 2004. In an eerily similar echo of the benzos' original launch, these new agents were touted as the answer to every insomniac's prayer--safe, effective, and restorative--with fewer side effects to bite their users.

In the lab, at least, there was reason for optimism. The Z drugs bound to only one of the five GABA receptors hit by benzodiazepines. "Ambien and its cousins are more selective," says Michel Cramer Bornemann, M.D., director of the Minnesota Regional Sleep Disorders Center. "This selectivity means they are cleaner drugs, minimizing the potential for addiction and tolerance."

Nevertheless, as early as 2002, the medical literature reported bizarre sleep behaviors linked to Z drugs. That year, case studies published in Sleep Medicine documented "sleepeating" in Ambien patients, some of whom had no memory of their nocturnal feasts.

Frozen meat, salt sandwiches, and buttered cigarettes are just a few of the delicacies Dr. Cramer Bornemann has seen Ambien sleep-eaters wolf down unawares. "We've had people try to drink laundry bleach," he says. "I had one patient who found a tub of margarine on his kitchen counter the next morning. It was half gone with a spoon stuck in it."

Most sleep eaters, fortunately, don't go to such extremes. "The majority just eat high-calorie junk foods," says Michael Silber, M.D., a neurologist and the codirector of the Mayo Clinic's center for sleep medicine. "But they can do so without awareness or recollection the next day."

One woman, for example, regularly woke up in a bed trashed with candy bar wrappers and Popsicle sticks. She blamed her husband, convinced he was snacking in bed after she'd fallen asleep. Only after she had "inexplicably" gained 100 pounds did her doctors link the weight gain to her Ambien use.

In the years since my first stint on Ambien, I've been on and off the drug numerous times. For long stretches, I've managed to sleep fine without having to rely on a chemical sandman. Periodically, though, something triggers a new bout of insomnia--most recently, a job loss last winter. If the sleep problem persists longer than a few weeks, I ask my doctor for Ambien. I then take it until life's stressors settle down enough for me to once again sleep drug-free.

To be honest, insomnia isn't the only reason I like Ambien. I also enjoy how it makes me feel--not high, exactly, but relaxed, don't-givean- F, worry-free. Of course, these pleasant feelings disappear once I fall asleep, which is why I often find myself fighting off sleep--yes, resisting the drug's intended effect--so I can enjoy the buzz a bit longer. For the most part, I do make it into my bedroom at the right time. I don't, however, always climb directly into bed.

How many licentious propositions I've dispatched under Ambien's influence is hard to say. Women who've sent morning-after Facebook "defriendings," I suspect, represent only a tiny fraction of the offended. It's not that I don't know by now the nature of the e-mails I'll end up sending. It's just that in the deepening bliss of Mr. Hyde's emergence, I can't imagine anyone objecting.

Clearly, I'm hardly alone in blurring the line between the therapeutic and recreational uses of Z drugs. Addiction researchers report that Ambien, and its generic versions in particular, are gaining cachet among drug abusers. Resisting the sedative effects, these users claim, triggers mild euphoria and visual distortions bordering on hallucination.

The drugs can also slash inhibitions, most famously the sexual variety. "The effect here can be quite potent for many," says W. Christopher Winter, M.D., a Men's Health advisor and the medical director of the sleep medicine center at Martha Jefferson Hospital, in Charlottesville, Virginia. In his practice he's seen die-hard prudes turn wanton under Ambien's influence. "One of my patients was a conservative woman, the kind who whispered every time she said the word 'sex.' But on Ambien, she became aggressive and quite explicit in her commands to her husband. She would physically force him to give her what she wanted." Ambien hasn't exactly put a Tiger in my own tank. It can, however, nudge an otherwise ordinary encounter ever so slightly in the direction of a wet dream.

Having sex while you're buzzed on Ambien, regardless of how clear the memory of it may or may not be the following day, is not the same as what clinicians call "sexsomnia." This latter condition, literally "sex while sleeping," is neither recreational nor conscious. A sexsomniac is no more in control of his or her actions than a sleepwalker is. The disorder has another distinctive feature: Episodes almost always occur close to home, if not within the person's own bed.

To understand what's going on here, consider the dual nature of the human brain. In our primitive midbrain, sometimes referred to as the reptilian brain, we have specialized neurons called "central pattern generators" that trigger reflex behaviors. "Reptiles are coldblooded predators," Dr. Cramer Bornemann says, "and to conserve heat, they prefer not to chase prey but rather to sit someplace warm and wait for prey to come to them."

When a rat accidentally wanders within a snake's strike zone, the reptile's central pattern generators recognize the stimuli and launch a strike. But mammals, from dogs to men, have also evolved a higher brain region that reptiles lack: an intelligent forebrain capable of inhibiting these central pattern generators. When we sleep, so does our forebrain, and there goes our capacity for self-restraint.

Normally this isn't a problem because, well, we're asleep. But in people who are vulnerable to parasomnia--abnormal behavior acted out while sleeping--a subtle environmental stimulus can rouse the reptilian brain without waking the intelligent brain, says Dr. Cramer Bornemann. Such stimuli can come in many forms, from a noise in the night to a sleep partner's touch. Z drugs, some researchers suspect, may promote parasomnia because of their selective action on our two different brains. They are particularly effective at keeping our intelligent faculties asleep--but much less so our primitive reptilian ones.

The person experiencing a parasomnia event is thus left with deep-seated, primitive impulses--from hunger and libido to self-defense and even aggression--all without the logic or foresight necessary to control the expression of those impulses. To an observer, a man in such a state can seem truly zombielike, a vacant-eyed automaton moving in semipurposeful ways in pursuit of unfathomable goals that have meaning only to him. Though outright violence is rare among such night wanderers, it almost always occurs when a well-meaning person tries to restrain the parasomniac from going after what he or she wants. Central pattern generators do not recognize loved ones; they see only obstacles to the fulfillment of needs. It is for this reason that sleep drugs, and Ambien in particular, have not escaped scrutiny by the legal system.

Dr. Winter recalls an incident in which one man took Ambien and was later arrested for disturbing the peace outside a Wendy's. It was 2 a.m.; the restaurant was closed, and the man was in his underwear, barefoot. "He told the cops he was mad because they took his order but wouldn't give him the food," Dr. Winter says. In another case, a man who took Ambien fell asleep watching TV with his girlfriend and her kids. He woke up on the couch several hours later in a spooning position with the 12-yearold daughter. He went to the bathroom and vomited from self-disgust. He was later charged with fondling her, but was acquitted after Dr. Cramer Bornemann testified that the man's actions took place while he was asleep.

Over the past 3 years, Dr. Cramer Bornemann has evaluated 52 separate criminal cases in which defendants blamed their actions on prescription drugs. "Fifty of those 52 cases have involved Ambien," he says. "Now does this mean there is something unique about Ambien? Or is it just a reflection of its very high prescription rate?"

Dr. Winter, for his part, is betting on the former. "The politically correct view is that all the Z drugs can cause parasomnia problems," he says, "but I think Ambien is clearly the king." A search of the medical literature, he adds, turns up dozens of references to Ambien-triggered parasomnia events, but few, if any, linked to Lunesta or Sonata (although Sonata has been implicated in a few overdoses). Why Ambien is worse--if in fact it is--may have to do with subtle differences in its chemical structure, which have been linked to other unusual effects.

A case in point is the recently discovered "zolpidem challenge test." Certain types of schizophrenia can leave patients catatonic and unable to speak. "But if you give these patients Ambien," says Dr. Cramer Bornemann, "the catatonia lifts temporarily and they can speak again. Most people think Ambien just puts people to sleep, but here is a clear case where it is actually stimulating." Who knows what other paradoxical behaviors it might produce?

In the wake of Ambien's FDA approval, sleep-drug prescriptions have risen dramatically. From 2002 to 2006, sales climbed 138 percent, with the overall market reaching $4 billion. The number of individual users, however, is harder to calculate. Frustrated by the lack of good data, Dr. Kripke filed Freedom of Information requests with the FDA, the Drug Enforcement Administration, and U.S. Customs. Z drugs and benzodiazepines alike are classified as Schedule IV controlled substances under federal law. Surely, Dr. Kripke believed, federal officials would have some idea of how many people were taking them.

"The agencies stalled," he says. "Only when threatened with legal action did they finally state to us that they did not have the information." Dr. Kripke acknowledges that Z drugs may be "somewhat less addicting" than benzodiazepines and barbiturates, but he says they can still cause addiction, and in the same way as those other drugs do. "Considering this, I think our government ought to pay better attention."

Whatever the number of users is, Z drug makers appear convinced that there's huge room for growth. Prime-time TV has become a dreamscape of commercials in which drowsy luminescent moths flap gently into the good night and annoying roosters are silenced by a tiny tablet.

Viewers hoping to live such dreams themselves do need a doctor's prescription, although this often proves just a formality. Many primary-care physicians today are so pressed for time that it's easier for them to write a scrip than to counsel a patient about good sleep hygiene--a drug-free behavioral approach that, studies show, is at least as effective as pills.

The doctor takes one look, diagnoses 'blood coming-out-of- your-ear syndrome,' and packs the patient's ear with cotton. The next week, even more blood is gushing out. Further stoking patient demand for chemical fixes are free-trial offers for Ambien CR and Lunesta, available in 4-day and 1-week supplies, respectively. You don't have to be a cynic to suspect that big money fuels these relentless recruitment campaigns.

"Before the original version of Ambien lost patent protection in 2007, the total Ambien franchise--Ambien and Ambien CR--was worth about $1.9 billion annually in the United States," says Robert Hazlett, a pharmaceuticals analyst for BMO Capital Markets. More than half of that came from Ambien. After the patent expiration of regular Ambien, Sanofi-aventis shifted its promotional focus to Ambien CR, the extended-release formulation that is still under patent protection. CR generated $900 million in 2008, while Ambien brought in $100 million. Though the difference in efficacy between the CR version and generic zolpidem is small, the difference in cost is not.

At my local chain-store pharmacy, a 30-day supply of Ambien CR retails for $201.97, or $6.73 a pill. A month of generic zolpidem, on the other hand, costs $18, or 60 cents per pill. Lunesta and Sonata go for $7 and $5 a pill. Granted, you might find better deals by shopping around, but even at a discount, is chemical sleep worth it?

Consider Sonata, a short-acting hypnotic in the same class as Ambien. The fine print on its manufacturer's own prescribing information concedes that "a significant difference from placebo on sleep duration was not demonstrated." A leading national consumer watchdog group, the Washington, D.C.-based Public Citizen, noted this and put Sonata in its "Do Not Use" drug category. The organization's health-research group rated Lunesta as not much better, declaring that it had no unique benefits, was costly, and was linked to cancer in animal studies.

On the other hand, a 2007 study published in the peer-reviewed journal Sleep determined that Lunesta improved sleep, quality of life, and work performance in insomnia patients when compared with a placebo. Moreover, these benefits continued after 6 months of nightly treatment. An earlier study published in Sleep Medicine reported similar sleep and daytime function benefits after a full year, with no evidence of users developing a tolerance.

It's important to note, however, that both studies received funding from Lunesta's manufacturer, Sepracor. In fact, six of the coauthors in the Sleep study were listed as employees of the company. Four other "independent" authors acknowledged, in lengthy disclosure statements, that they had consulting arrangements and/or had received research support from drug-company sponsors.

Corporate influence in clinical trials is a hot topic in biomedical ethics. Some academic studies have found little or no bias, but many others conclude otherwise. In 2003, for example, a Yale meta-analysis published in the Journal of the American Medical Association revealed that industry-sponsored medical research is 3.6 times as likely to reach a pro-industry conclusion as are studies lacking such ties.

"Most of the people who are speaking in favor of the current hypnotics have received money from the hypnotics companies. That's an obvious fact," says Dr. Kripke. "Whether their opinions are being distorted by their financial interest is something that's hard to prove. But it's hard to bite the hand that feeds you."

Following the FDA's 2007 decision to beef up the sleep drugs' labels with additional warnings, other concerns have emerged. In 2008, Dr. Kripke published a review of 15 separate epidemiological trials that suggested that the latest hypnotics may increase cancer risk, particularly skin cancer. "Though the mechanisms for cancer risk are not well understood," says Dr. Kripke, "these drugs are clastogenic, which means they can break chromosomes. Chromosome breaks are one well-known mechanism for causing cancer. But we don't know if this is what's going on with Z drugs."

Another meta-analysis published by Dr. Kripke in 2009 found an association between use of the drugs and infection rates, particularly upper-respiratory tract infections. One possible explanation: Sleep-med users are too conked out to swallow, allowing microbes to avoid eviction.

Z drugs may also play a role in another threat to men's health: GERD, or gastroesophageal reflux disease. GERD is caused by acid splashing up from the stomach into the esophagus. It's particularly common during sleep because of your body's prone position. Over time, chronic acid exposure can narrow the sufferer's esophagus and can increase his risk of esophageal cancer. This cancer, more common in men than women, is deadly; its 5-year survival rate is less than 20 percent.

Researchers at Thomas Jefferson University, in Philadelphia, conducted a clinical trial to find out if Ambien may increase the risk of more severe cases of GERD. During normal sleep, people reflexively swallow refluxed stomach acid. And the researchers preliminary data suggests that Z drugs are blunting this automatic response. This, in turn, may prolong acid contact with esophageal tissues, worsening the disease in those already diagnosed--and possibly triggering it in others.

The pursuit of sleep, in other words, may well be making you sick to your stomach. Skin and other cancers, respiratory infections, GERD: There isn't enough evidence for new warnings. Then again, Ambien was on the market for almost 15 years before drugmakers and the FDA took "complex sleep-related behaviors" seriously.

In its consensus statement about the treatment of chronic insomnia, a National Institutes of Health panel conceded that mankind still knows very little about the condition. Even for prescription-drug and other treatments that have been systematically evaluated, the panel expressed concern "about the mismatch between the potential lifelong nature of this illness and the longest clinical trials, which have lasted 1 year or less."

In the meantime, might still other long-term consequences only now be starting to emerge?

"It reminds me of 1950s magazine ads for sleeping pills," says Dr. Winter. "Some of those pills are considered too dangerous to use anymore. We might not realize the potential problems with current drugs. How will people 40 years from now view our sleep meds?"

Like me, my dad was prone to occasional spells of insomnia, and like me, he found that his sleep quality and duration both took a gradual downturn with each passing decade after adolescence. Before he passed away, he gave me some sleep advice I've never forgotten.

"There are two kinds of sleepers--those who wake up in the middle of the night and worry about it, and those who wake up in the middle of the night and don't worry about it. But every guy I know wakes up sometimes. That's just how we are. I've decided not to worry about it."

Better late than never, I'm taking his advice.

How to Take a Sleeping Pill

Step 1 Do the math Can you devote 8 hours to sleep? If not, don't take a Z drug that night--otherwise you'll still be under the influence in the a.m., says Men's Health sleep medicine advisor W. Christopher Winter, M.D. The exception: Sonata, which lasts only 4 hours.

Step 2 Pick your pill Each Z drug has its strengths. "Sonata is for people who wake during the night and need to fall back asleep," says Dr. Winter. Low-dose (1 milligram) Lunesta does the same. Ambien and 2-milligram Lunesta induce sleep, while Ambien CR and 3-milligram Lunesta help you sack out and stay out.

Step 3 Skip dessert The drugs work best on an empty stomach. "Food slows their absorption," says Dr. Winter. "Depending on the meal, the effect may be delayed several hours."

Step 4 Create a cocoon Silence your cellphone, close the blinds, and insert earplugs. "If some small stimulus partially awakens you, you may suddenly be making a sandwich out of bread and cigarettes," says Dr. Winter.

Step 5 Pop and drop "After taking a sleep aid, you may initially be able to stay awake, but the next morning you probably won't remember anything you did," says Dr. Winter. "Take the pill as you're literally sitting on the side of the bed."

Step 6 Know when to quit After a month, you should be able to sleep pill-free, says Dr. Winter. If you can't, your doctor should investigate possible causes, such as stress, sleep apnea, or even restless legs syndrome.

iPad Insomnia?

The source of all that tossing and turning may be sitting right on your lap. In a recent study from Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston, researchers found that dim light, such as the light emitted from a laptop, iPad, e-reader, or smart-phone, could suppress your body's production of the sleep hormone melatonin. The net effect: You feel more wired than tired. "Lowlevel light can stimulate special nonvisual photoreceptor cells in the retina, ultimately making you more alert and increasing your temperature and heart rate," says study author Steven W. Lockley, Ph.D. If you've been finding that you just can't conk out, try this experiment: A half hour before bedtime, turn off any electronics that have lighted screens. And yes, a 52-inch LCD TV hanging on your bedroom wall counts as a "lighted screen."
