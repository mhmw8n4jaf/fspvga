# The 10 Best Modern War Movies

THE DEBATE OVER whether war films, by depicting the act of warfare, are automatically guilty of glorifying it—in other words, over whether it’s even possible for a film to be “anti-war”—has existed as long as cinema itself, and it continues to rage today.

The ideological schism at the heart of war movies is perhaps best distilled by the opposing viewpoints espoused by two of our great filmmakers. In an interview in 1973, Francois Truffaut famously told Gene Siskel that “every film about war ends up being pro-war,” positing that the mere action of dramatizing violent conflict runs the risk of glamorizing it, given cinema’s visual and narrative propensity to render it as spectacle. (Case in point: even movies exploring the moral ambiguity and dehumanizing effects of a conflict like the Vietnam War, such as Full Metal Jacket and Apocalypse Now, exaggerate hyper-masculine dynamics—with their grim gallows humor and ultra-violent rites of passage—at play between their brothers-in-arms, making for fascinatingly queasy entertainment.)

Having directed Saving Private Ryan, Steven Spielberg countered Truffaut’s claim by stating in an interview around the time of its release that “every war movie, good or bad, is an anti-war movie.” Depicting the violence of combat up-close, Spielberg seemed to say, serves to frame war’s alleged greater good in intensely personal terms, making it impossible for the audience to distance themselves from the brutality of conflict; Come and See, which he screened before making Schindler’s List, comes to mind in its hellish depiction of atrocities perpetrated during Nazi occupation. War is hell, the saying goes, and this line of logic suggests that realistically portraying conflict makes inarguable its horrors.

With their ruthlessly effective thriller Warfare (now playing in theaters, via A24), co-writers/directors Ray Mendoza and Alex Garland land somewhere between these two schools of thought. Based on Mendoza’s experiences in Iraq as a former U.S. Navy SEAL, the film follows a platoon of SEALs on a surveillance mission gone wrong, who find themselves under siege in insurgent territory. Emphasizing viscerally immersive realism over wider political context, while recruiting a who’s-who of young male leads—D’Pharaoh Woon-A-Tai, Will Poulter, Kit Connor, Joseph Quinn, Cosmo Jarvis, Michael Gandolfini, and Charles Melton, among others—to play the soldiers, this boots-on-the-ground story of military coordination and camaraderie in crisis is just the latest skirmish into a stimulating debate over what cinema can achieve in depicting warfare.

In time for Warfare’s release, Men’s Health is running down recent depictions of military combat in film to evaluate the “best” modern war films, though any such ranking necessitates first a set of caveats and disclaimers. Though recent films continue to explore historical conflicts from a variety of angles (from ultraviolent WWII pastiche Inglourious Basterds to Blitz, an astonishing vision of the social prejudices at play in London circa 1940, and Dunkirk, a sensory immersion into the beachfront evacuation that took place that same year), these types of films were not eligible for this ranking. Only narrative features from the past 25 years that depict the realities of combat in modern geopolitical conflicts in approximately this same time-span were considered.

From gut-wrenching frontline dispatches like Restrepo and Retrograde (essential chronicles of U.S. involvement in Afghanistan) to accounts of civilians caught in crossfire, like For Sama (covering five years of a woman’s life in Aleppo, Syria) and From Ground Zero (22 shorts by Palestinian directors living in the besieged Gaza Strip), war documentaries are separately essential. As Ukraine endures Russian invasion, docs like 20 Days in Mariupol and Rule of Two Walls show civilians fighting to survive; contextualizing the Israel-Palestine conflict, this year's Academy Award winner for Best Documentary Feature, No Other Land, documents Israel’s strategic, decades-long campaign of violence and displacement against Palestinian communities in the occupied West Bank.

Mention should be made of critiques of the U.S. military-industrial complex, like Fahrenheit 9/11 and Taxi to the Dark Side, and of exposés like The Invisible War, uncovering a crisis of sexual violence within the U.S. military. Equally bracing films, like The Fog of War and The Act of Killing, offer thought-provoking probes of the darker side of human nature. As recent films have grappled with America’s War on Terror, and with the unending assaults on Iraq and Afghanistan that defined U.S. foreign policy for two decades, that underlying question (“War movies: what are they good for?”) continues to trouble and compel filmmakers working to portray modern combat on screen.

Below, Men’s Health examines some of the most thought-provoking answers they’ve posed to date.

The Hurt Locker (2009)

Kathryn Bigelow’s nail-biting military procedural, which won the Oscar for Best Picture, follows a U.S. Army Explosive Ordnance Disposal team through the second year of the Iraq War, zeroing in on the psychology of the staff sergeants and specialists tasked with detecting and defusing weapons of war. An incendiary pressure-cooker of a picture, it’s also an insightful portrait of the paranoia and dread that accompanies such an existentially terrifying occupation. Without ever short-changing the heroism of the bomb-disposal experts who put themselves in harm’s way to protect their fellow military-service members, Bigelow—and screenwriter Mark Boal, who based the film on his experiences while embedded with an American bomb squad in Iraq—don’t hide from the difficult reality that certain soldiers, forged under such fraught circumstances, become addicted to the rush. One of the most significant films made about the Iraq War, The Hurt Locker is all about the futility of that mindset, its anguish and tragedy; it emphasizes the purgatorial existence of soldiers—trapped in endless, asymmetrical battle with an enemy they can’t see—who sense danger everywhere they look until they’re consumed by it. Despite their awareness it will all eventually blow up in their faces, “war is a drug.” That opening quotation, from journalist Chris Hedges’s book War Is A Force That Gives Us Meaning, describes a certain moral conditioning that justifies conflict’s primal purpose and—when elevated politically—leaves entire cultures hooked. That such an assessment applies not only to those on the ground but to decision-makers back in D.C., giddy for others to rush into battle, is what makes The Hurt Locker such an unsettling document of American war-time pathology.

Da 5 Bloods (2020)

When the four veterans (Delroy Lindo, Clarke Peters, Norm Lewis, Isiah Whitlock Jr.) at the heart of Spike Lee’s post-Vietnam War epic travel back to Ho Chi Minh City in search of gold they’d looted while serving there, what follows is a haunting, vivid, impassioned reckoning with American history, the relative value it ascribes to Black lives, and the inextricability of both these concepts from its characters’ fraught senses of self. While the film recounts their war-time sacrifices via flashback combat sequences (which Lee imbues with imagery that provocatively evokes a vast cinematic lineage of Westerns and war films so as to illuminate the racial blindspots that both contained), Da 5 Bloods is ultimately about the way the war endures, in the hearts and minds of those it transformed and traumatized. The shadow of Vietnam, in all its grievance and horror and cognitive dissonance, looms impossibly large over its characters, as they find themselves reliving their experiences—and time serving under a heroic fallen leader (Chadwick Boseman)—in a psychic loop of longing, despair, and disenfranchisement. Since its release on Netflix in 2020, and certainly in light of our current political crisis, Lee’s stinging conflagration—melding social critique, self-contradicting psychology, wartime action, and history lesson with an insistence on their complicated linkage—seems only to have intensified in its prophetic power.

The only filmmaker with two movies on this list, Kathryn Bigelow followed up The Hurt Locker by dramatizing the decade-long international manhunt for Osama bin Laden, leader of the terrorist group al-Qaeda, after the September 11 attacks. With Zero Dark Thirty, though, she goes further than historiography, instead subverting the lone-wolf heroism that epitomizes American military machismo in contemporary cinema through her steadfast focus on a female CIA analyst (Jessica Chastain) whose steely, matter-of-fact determination ultimately does more to bring about bin Laden’s death than the military’s use of torture. Bigelow—reteaming with Hurt Locker scribe Mark Boal—does not gloss over the brutish tactics implemented by military operatives in their decade-long hunt, nor does she glorify them, instead interrogating use of force through grim, protracted sequences of torture that—from the film’s opening minutes—make it a story about moral compromise and its corrosive psychological cost. In combat, this terrible calculus is an ever-present tension; that Zero Dark Thirty explores it in this specific context makes it one of the most fraught, fascinating depictions of virtue ethics in modern warfare.

Sicario (2015)

Much like the war on terror, the “war on drugs” has continuously engaged the U.S. government in a sweeping military campaign, both offensive and defensive, against an ill-defined enemy; In the process, both wars have jeopardized not only the lives of soldiers but the civil liberties of those caught up in what could arguably be called an existential conflict. In its gripping, morally opaque depiction of an FBI agent (Emily Blunt) who joins forces with two task-force operatives (Benicio del Toro and Josh Brolin) to hunt a cartel chief south of the U.S.-Mexico border, Denis Villeneuve’s scorching procedural thriller shows the dark reality of a drug war waged without ethical, emotional, or psychological borders. In what it has to say about the constant threat a corrupt system poses to the moral actors it encounters, and about the omniscience of violence in a conflict with no agreed-upon rules of engagement, Sicario is a war film about unchecked American aggression coming home to roost and, in that, a deeply moral story about the waking terror of war that has become an end unto itself.

Beasts of No Nation (2015)

Telling the story of a West African orphan (Abraham Attah) who’s pressed into service as a child soldier at the behest of a dangerous warlord (Idris Elba), Cary Joji Fukunaga’s Beasts of No Nation—adapting the book of the same name by Uzodinma Iweala—depicts the human cost of conflict with unsparingly brutal yet often surreally heightened lyricism. As it charts its main character’s transformation into a murderer, in the midst of his unnamed country’s devastating civil war, Beasts of No Nation grows increasingly eerie and feverish, mirroring an individual’s loss of innocence within the more ghastly and atmospheric descent into hell that unending war seems to wreak on his entire world.

American Sniper (2014)

A far more complicated picture than the jingoistic pro-war propaganda it was painted as at the time of its initial release, Clint Eastwood’s portrait of the U.S. Navy SEAL Chris Kyle (Bradley Cooper)—known as the deadliest sniper in U.S. military history, killed at a shooting range by a traumatized fellow veteran four years after his honorable discharge—is an up-close character study of a man’s psyche being slowly hollowed out by the violence he inflicts. From this specific filmmaker, such subject matter is familiar territory, but Eastwood capably translates his abiding career interests in moral gray areas of masculinity, the trickiness of heroism, and the violence at the core of American identity into an Iraq War drama that finds in its protagonist a microcosm of the conflict in which his legacy was made, to which his life was given. American Sniper, which reaches unbearable levels of tension in various shootout sequences during Kyle’s deployment, even fashions him a foil in an Iraqi sniper (Sammy Sheik) he becomes hell-bent on eliminating, despite the risks this all-consuming obsession pose for his fellow service members. In the thick of a conflict as ideologically muddled and endlessly brutal as Iraq, Kyle finds terrible purpose—which sets up the film’s haunting third act, in which he returns home to find he’s not quite ready to leave the war behind. One of Eastwood’s most thought-provoking pictures, it depicts a man being made into a lethal weapon, then stays with him long enough to reckon with the fallout of his faltering attempts to reclaim a sense of humanity in the country he was asked to surrender it for.

Good Kill (2014)

Another intense, thought-provoking drama about the War on Terror’s direct and collateral damage, this one is intimately focused on a drone pilot (Ethan Hawke) who facilitates strikes over Afghanistan from a military base near his home in Nevada. That this pilot has come into the position after five deployments is only part of what troubles him; At first, the misgivings he feels about remotely piloting these unmanned aerial vehicles involves his lack of personal investment in the work, relative to the years he spent flying fighter-bombers. As his new assignment continues, however, the onslaught of violence he perpetuates—and the moral ambiguity that creeps into the missions he’s assigned by CIA controllers, which can involve attacks on civilian targets in countries where the U.S. has no clearly defined military mission, begins to take a steep psychological toll. Modern warfare, with evolving weapons technology and capabilities, has created new types of soldiers; The drone program depicted in Good Kill involves one such evolution, and the film especially excels in exploring the ethical considerations required of such “virtual warriors.”

Civil War (2024)

In Civil War, “shoot first, ask questions later,” is a mantra shared by soldiers, photojournalists, and perhaps filmmakers as well, as implied by the emphatically ground-level perspective that writer-director Alex Garland adopts to embed audiences on a haunting tour of a modern-day America torn asunder by civil war. Garland—with Warfare co-writer/co-director Ray Mendoza involved as a military advisor—largely elides the specifics of the conflict consuming the nation in his vision of its hellish near-future; This decision attracted controversy at the time of the film’s release, but it aligns with the film’s perspective on the slow ethical erosion of its photojournalist protagonists, all of whom at least endeavor to keep their own ideology at arm’s length in favor of a futile, self-defeating objectivity. Garland is not subtle about proposing this impulse as the very problem we face in a politically polarized landscape; If we allow ourselves to become inured to the atrocity we witness, we risk internalizing a type of apathy that threatens our ability to pierce through the fog of war. Do we resign ourselves to horror? At what point does bearing witness become participation?

Guy Ritchie's The Covenant (2023)

When the U.S. military withdrew from Afghanistan in 2021, it turned its back on thousands of Afghan interpreters who had risked their lives to assist soldiers on the ground. In the chaotic aftermath of this exit, as the U.S. failed to keep its promise to provide visas to allow interpreters and their families to resettle in the U.S., the Taliban retaliated by torturing and killing hundreds of them. While fictional, The Covenant focuses on the bond between a U.S. military sergeant (Jake Gyllenhaal) and his interpreter (Dar Salim), dramatizing the existential debt incurred by wartime brotherhood—a debt that becomes especially palpable once the sergeant is injured and returns home, only to battle past layers of bureaucracy to secure safe passage out of Afghanistan for the interpreter and his family. In foregrounding this relationship, and its endurance in defiance of an American military apparatus that often treated real soldiers and interpreters as expendable, the film is made with sobering restraint—despite agonizingly tense battle sequences, including an ambush at a major weapons cache that, explosively, marks The Covenant’s transition from a standard-issue action thriller into a more sensitive story of survival, honor, and sacrifice.

The Outpost (2020)

Among the lesser-seen films on this list, Rod Lurie’s gut-wrenching depiction of the real-life Battle of Kamdesh—one of the bloodiest battles involving U.S. troops in Afghanistan—keeps its focus squarely on the soldiers who struggle to survive an assault by hundreds of Taliban fighters on their base, which was located in a mountain valley near the border of Pakistan. That the base never should have been established there in the first place, given the immense tactical advantage it ceded to an enemy that could dominate their position from higher ground, becomes catastrophically obvious once the shooting starts; Four officers in the chain of command were later disciplined for inadequately supporting the base, though Pentagon officials whose policy set the tone for this strategy escaped such accountability. Indefensible though the outpost was, its soldiers defended it gallantly. With an admirable authenticity in depicting not only weapons handling and use of cover in firefights but also the way the soldiers’ interpersonal dynamics inform their decision-making in the chaos of combat, The Outpost—on which Jariko Denman, a retired U.S. Army Ranger, worked alongside Warfare’s Ray Mendoza as both military technical advisor and associate producer—is a film that honors their sacrifice with unflinching realism.
