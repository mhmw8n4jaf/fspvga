# Adam Driver Isn't Exactly Sure What 'Toxic Masculinity' Is

Adam Driver has been hailed as an unconventional new kind of leading man by Hollywood, thanks to a series of performances which range from the comic to the compelling. He has played all manner of men in his career, from his breakout role as the intensely odd love interest in HBO's Girls to the brooding villain Kylo Ren in the new Star Wars trilogy, and he has portrayed nuanced, human complexity in each of these roles. But in a recent profile by The New Yorker, Driver admitted that he's not exactly au fait with the discourse around masculinity.

Throughout his lengthy conversations with Michael Schulman, Driver recalled how how his conservative religious upbringing meant that he wasn't exposed to a lot of popular culture until later on in his life, including the 1999 movie Fight Club. Schulman writes:

Part of him still feels blindsided, as if he’d missed a class and hadn’t yet caught up on the wider world. While discussing "Fight Club," he asked what I thought of the movie, I said that I hadn’t seen it in years but wondered how it would play in an era when people are hyperaware of toxic masculinity."What do you mean, 'toxic masculinity'?" he asked.I suggested that male aggression is seen as less purifying now than it may have been portrayed as being in "Fight Club." "I’d have to think about it," Driver said. "I mean, I haven’t heard much about toxic masculinity." He chuckled. "Maybe because I’m part of the problem!"

His comments are somewhat surprising, given his involvement in movies like Spike Lee's BlacKkKlansman, a satire which was steeped in social issues.

Later on, Driver elaborated on what he meant, describing how as his own relationship with religion and authority has evolved, he has a harder time keeping up with the cultural conversation, because he has developed a mistrust of being told what to believe. A result of this is that, as an increasingly famous actor in a huge franchise, Driver ends up being both a part of the discourse, and completely separate from it.

"For a lot of times in my life, I was told there was a right answer," he said. "And then, when I got older, I was, like, That’s fucking total bullshit. I feel that very much with acting, too. If you knew how to do it, you would do it perfectly every time... So anytime anyone tells me, This is the right answer, or There’s something called toxic masculinity, I’m, like, What? What are you talking about? I’m skeptical of it, because I feel like I was duped for seventeen years of my life."
