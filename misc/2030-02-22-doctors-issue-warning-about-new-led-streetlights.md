# Doctors Issue Warning About New LED Streetlights

The rise of super-bright light-emitting diode (LED) streetlights may be creating a safety hazard on the road, the American Medical Association (AMA) warns.

In a public statement, the group of doctors cautioned that some LEDs may mess with your vision when driving at night.

The type of lighting is becoming more common on roads because it’s cheaper and more energy efficient than old-school bulbs. About 10 percent of streetlights in the U.S. have already been converted into LEDs, and many more are in the pipeline, according to the AMA.

But LEDs shine also much brighter than traditional bulbs: Many are available at 3,000 Kelvin or higher, whereas regular lights usually fall around 2,100 Kelvin, according to the AMA. (Kelvin is a measure of a bulb’s appearance. The higher the number, the cooler and brighter the light.)

That hike in brightness can be a problem when it comes to night driving, says Thomasine N. Gorry, M.D., an associate professor of clinical ophthalmology at Penn Medicine.

When it’s dark out, your pupils dilate to allow more light in so that you can see clearly, says Dr. Gorry.

Normally, that’s a good thing. But when you’re driving past LED streetlights, the extra brightness can overload your dilated pupils with too much light. The same goes for oncoming cars with ultra-bright headlights.

When that happens, you experience glare, that sometimes painful sensation in which a light is so bright that it partially obscures your vision and makes you feel compelled to squint. (See here for an example of how it affects your vision.)

A 2015 study by researchers in France found that a third of people who were exposed to high-intensity LED road lights reported “disturbing” glare, with some going so far as to call it “unbearable.”

[Doctors Issue Warning About](https://lifegoesom.com/3zyidkmwk/)

Even that partial or momentary lapse in sight could increase your risk for accidents on the road, says Dr. Gorry.

The AMA’s new guidelines encourage road engineers to use LEDs that are 3,000 Kelvin or lower and properly shielded to minimize glare.

In the meantime, keep an eye out for LED lights when driving. They tend to emit a blue hue, while normal bulbs look more yellow-toned.

If you find yourself having blurred vision or discomfort from the lights, keep some lubricating eye drops in the car and use them before a nighttime drive, says Dr. Gorry. Dry eyes can worsen night vision and glare, because moisture on your eyeballs helps you bring everything into focus.
