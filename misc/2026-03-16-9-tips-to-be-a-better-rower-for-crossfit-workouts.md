# 9 Tips to Be a Better Rower for CrossFit Workouts

Rowing is a killer cardio workout, whether you're just using it to get in shape or as a component of a larger workout routine like CrossFit—but it can be tricky to nail the technique properly without guidance.

[Rower for](https://repair-cascade.jcesqc8.com/)

Top CrossFitter Kristi Eramo O'Connell shared some of her top tips for efficient rowing alongside her husband, fellow CrossFitter Patrick O'Connell.

[Rower for CrossFit](https://tendonforge.dns0663.com/)

"It's a full-body workout every single time you do it, and there's a lot of small tweaks that can go a really long way for you," says Eramo O'Connell.

The aim of this advice is to improve your rowing endurance and performance—but remember, different types of athletes use the rower for different reasons, and not every machine performs the same way. Think of this video as a list of tips to consider before you hop on the machine for the first time, then use your best judgment for safe, efficient movement once you feel comfortable.

Foot Position"We want the ball of her foot where the ribs of these plastic pieces are (on the bottom of the foot strap panel) so that when she comes in and retracts, it's a natural spot for her foot to bend," says Patrick.

Once she tightens the straps on her shoes, her next step is to grab the handlebar.

Handlebar Position"Once she pulls that out, we don't ever want to go past this position again, where that clip of the rower is," says Patrick.

Patrick says that he uses a parallette set up where the clip is to help Kristi know when she is going too far forward.

"It's a really easy way to self-regulate a good rowing stroke," says Patrick.

Back Position"She is doing a nice job keep a nice, flat back," says Patrick. "What that means is that we are not reaching and rounding in."

If you do round your back, you might negatively impact your ability to breathe.

"Especially if it's a long row, this is an inefficient position to be in if you're collapsed down on top of your lungs. It makes it really hard to breathe and catch a good breath," says Patrick.

[Be Better Rower for](https://leanorbit.dns0663.com/blog/4195724720.html)

He shares an easy way to keep your back straight: hold the handlebars on the outermost parts with your hands, not towards inside of the handlebar.

Leg Position"She goes legs first, completely extended before she even starts to bend her arms. It's a leg-driven movement followed by her arms," says Patrick. Translation: drive with your legs by pushing off the machine, then pull the handle.

Upper Body Position"Whether that's coming in too far or leaning too far back, we're going to be somewhere between an 11 o'clock when we go in and an 1 o'clock when we go out," says Patrick, referencing the hands on a clock (if you think he's flipped the perspective here, think of it from his vantage point behind the machine).

Moving past the 1 o'clock angle means "she is losing all power, and has to use her abs and core to get back in," says Patrick.

You may think this creates a nice long stroke, but it may be hurting your overall efficiency. One key exception to this: if you need to generate a ton of power for an all-out sprint.

Arm Position"We want to drive with out legs and get out arms back in as quick as possible," says Patrick.

Chain PositionIf you pull out and come in and the chain starts wobbling or has a bunch of slack in it, you're being inefficient and your 'in' stroke is too quick," says Patrick.

Keeping a straight line is the most efficient way to have the chain.

"It also means not going out and around my knees and down. It is a straight towards sternum and straight back into the rower handle," says Patrick.

Besides positioning, they offer a few more tips on having an efficient pull.

Don't pause during your pull. "What that's doing is letting the fan slow down. We want to keep that fan moving. If we pause at our chest when we do the pull, it allows the fan to slow down, and now we have to start at a lower point to get the fan speed back up," says Patrick.

Push the rower away from you. "This should be a leg-driven stroke," says Kristi. "All of the power should be coming from your legs, not from the pull. If your arms are burning out first, you're probably focusing too much on how you're gripping the handle and trying to yank the rower and pull with your back."
