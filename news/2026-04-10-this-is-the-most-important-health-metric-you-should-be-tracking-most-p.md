# This Is the Most Important Health Metric You Should Be Tracking. Most People Aren’t.

YOUR SLEEP SCORE is consistently high, you’re tracking macros like it’s your job, and you’ve finally got your V02 max and heart rate variability dialed in. Thanks to your fitness tracker, you’re healthier than ever.

Or are you?

“Wearables are great at measuring data related to athletic training and can help you improve various fitness metrics, like HRV,” says Christopher Kelly, MD, chairman of cardiology at the University of North Carolina Rex Hospital in Raleigh and a Men’s Health advisor. What most of them aren’t tracking is blood pressure. “That has a far greater effect on your health,” he says.

From an overall health perspective, having healthy blood pressure is far more of a flex than, say, a superior VO2 max. So why don’t most of us see it that way?

“Measuring blood pressure doesn’t have the instant gratification or coolness of fitness tracking,” says Rishi Wadhera, MD, a cardiologist at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and associate professor of medicine at Harvard Medicine. “But it’s the only way to assess the strain your blood vessels are under. Since hypertension doesn’t cause symptoms, it flies below the radar, quietly damaging the pipes that deliver blood to your body and brain and increasing your risk of being blindsided by a heart attack or stroke.”

At last, some popular wearables are aiming to put this essential metric on your radar, which may be a step toward raising this essential metric’s profile. The Series 11 Apple Watch, for instance, includes a hypertension detection algorithm that notifies you if it detects patterns that suggest your blood pressure might be high. It won’t give you numbers, but will suggest that you get it checked out. Whoop has a feature called Blood Pressure Insights that uses various bits of data (including heart rate, HRV, and blood flow patterns) to estimate your blood pressure. It’s not intended to be a medical device that diagnoses a condition, the company says, but to help you understand how your blood pressure affects your performance and how your lifestyle affects your blood pressure. While they’re not stand-ins for standard blood pressure monitoring yet, they are bringing eyes and minds to a critical number that just doesn’t get enough cred.

Why this metric matters so much

With each beat, your heart’s left ventricle pumps about one-third of a cup of nutrient- and oxygen-rich blood into your arteries, which range in size from the aorta, the largest, at up to nearly an inch in diameter, to the .01-millimeter arterioles, with a diameter that’s comparable to a particle of talcum powder. Even a slight decrease in artery size (which can be caused by multiple factors, including plaque build-up, smoking, fatty diets and chronic stress) can cause a large increase in resistance, forcing the heart to work harder to move blood around the body—like trying to drink a milk shake through a kids’ juice-box straw. The smaller your metaphorical straw, the higher your blood pressure.

That’s where things get dicey. “High blood pressure triples your risk of stroke and heart failure and doubles your risk of coronary artery disease,” Dr. Kelly explains. What’s more, hypertension can damage your kidneys by impairing healthy blood flow in the brain and hampering its ability to get rid of harmful waste products. Your brain doesn’t escape trouble, either. In August 2025, for the first time in eight years, the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology updated their blood pressure guidelines. “They now emphasize the link between high blood pressure and cognitive decline, because the evidence for that connection has become clearer,” says Daniel Jones, MD, chair of the guideline committee and past president of the American Heart Association.

Even the fittest among us may have problems, since hypertension can be genetic or stem from kidney problems or sleep apnea, and the risk typically increases with age. Fifty percent of adults—yes, half of us—in the U.S. will develop high blood pressure during their lifetime. “The odds are a coin toss that you’ll end up being one of them,” says Dr. Kelly.

How to track this overlooked metric

While the Whoop and Apple technologies are helpful right now in directing attention to this metric, the truth—and maybe this is part of BP’s image problem—is that the only way to get an accurate measurement is still with a blood pressure cuff. Yes, it’s decidedly old school and it can’t notify you when your DoorDash arrives. But it could potentially save your life.

At $40 to $50, a cuff is a lot more affordable than a smartwatch—and offers far more bang for the buck. “Home monitoring gives people easy access to this potentially life-saving health information,” says Dr. Jones. Of course, there’s no sense tracking your blood pressure once or twice a day with a device that’s not accurate, so check this list of scientifically validated ones before you buy, and check out these rules for tracking it right that Peter Attia, MD., went through for Men's Health.

Nearly everyone can benefit from home monitoring, because it can help you catch the condition early—but for some men it’s especially important. If you already know you have high blood pressure (even if you’re taking medication), you should monitor it on your own, as should anyone with risk factors including a family history of the condition, chronic kidney disease, obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea and poor diet. The condition is more common in Black, Hispanic and Asian men, and increases with age. According to the Centers for Disease Control, hypertension affects 30 percent of men ages 18 to 39, 56 percent of men 40 to 59, and 71 percent of those 60 and older. “Those odds alone indicate you should be monitoring yourself,” says Dr. Kelly.

What should you be aiming for

Healthy blood pressure is less than 120/80 mm Hg (shorthand for millimeters of mercury), “elevated” is 120-129/80mm Hg, and “high” is 130/80mm Hg. (The first number indicates your systolic pressure, the force exerted on your arteries when your heart pumps blood, and the second is the diastolic pressure, when your heart rests between beats.)

If yours is elevated or high, you can try lifestyle changes for three to six months. If that doesn’t work, you likely need medication.

The good news for hard-core tracking fanatics: Blood pressure is remarkably responsive to lifestyle changes, so once you’re keeping tabs on it you can see how small habit changes affect it over time, says Dr. Kelly. In other words, you can see how all those other metrics—like steps and sleep—affect your blood pressure.

Seemingly small drops make a big difference. For every 10 mm Hg reduction in systolic blood pressure, you reduce the risk of coronary heart disease by 17 percent, stroke by 27 percent and heart failure by 28 percent, according to the American College of Cardiology. “Getting blood pressure below 130 reduces dementia risk by 15 percent,” adds Dr. Jones.

How to lower your blood pressure

If there were a Strava for blood pressure, you’d want to use these strategies to get your KOM:

Exercise regularly

“It’s the single best treatment,” says Dr. Wadhera. The recommended 150 minutes a week of moderate exercise or 75 minutes of vigorous typically can lower systolic blood pressure up to 10 points in one to three months—and the benefits last as long as you keep moving. Strength training is effective, too—as are isometric exercises, like planks, squats and bridges. A meta-analysis published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2023 looked at 270 clinical trials, including more than 15,000 people, and found that isometric exercises were more effective at lowering blood pressure than aerobic, resistance or high-intensity interval exercise.

“For many men, losing 10 pounds can make the difference between elevated and normal blood pressure,” says Dr. Wadhera.

Substitute potassium for sodium

“We’ve long known that sodium contributes to hypertension, which is why we recommend having no more than a teaspoon of salt every day,” says Dr. Jones. But getting more potassium is part of the new guidelines, because the higher your potassium, the more sodium you excrete.” Researchers in China recently found that in people who had already had a stroke—and as a result were at increased risk of another one—using a potassium-enriched salt substitute lowered their blood pressure and significantly reduced the rate of future strokes.

Pay attention to alcohol

The new blood pressure recommendations are clear about this: Abstain from alcohol or strictly adhere to the long-standing rule of no more than two drinks a day. “Alcohol is a significant risk factor for high blood pressure,” says Dr. Kelly. “It initially lowers blood pressure, but as it wears off, you have a mini-withdrawal, with high adrenaline levels and elevated heart rate. Plus, it can cause weight gain, another risk factor.”
