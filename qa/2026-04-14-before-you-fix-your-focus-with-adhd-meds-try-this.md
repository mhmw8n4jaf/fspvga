# Before You Fix Your Focus with ADHD Meds, Try This

Doc, I can’t focus. Can I get a prescription for Ritalin?”

Lately, that’s the question I’ve been hearing from guys in our sessions. It happens several times a week. They’re convinced they have attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and that the drug—generally prescribed to kids with the issue, and famously abused by college students in an attempt to study better—will help them concentrate and propel them to a whole new level of productivity.

Take a patient I’ll call Cooper, a graphic designer in his mid-40s. Cooper was having trouble paying attention to detail; he’d lose his train of thought, forget where he put stuff, and always fidget with whatever was on his desk. He’d even started wearing his house keys on a lanyard around his neck so he wouldn’t lose them. “That’s the story of my life these days,” he said the first time he came to my office. Like many of my patients, Cooper was certain he had somehow developed ADHD as an adult and thought Ritalin would help.

Maybe, but not so fast. The truth is, ADHD isn’t all that common; it occurs in only about 9 percent of children and 5 percent of adults. Some psychiatrists argue that there’s adult-onset ADHD, but there isn’t enough evidence for me to get on board with that position. It’s possible that in some people the diagnosis was missed when they were kids. But more likely there’s something else going on.

Checking your phone a million times an hour doesn’t always mean you have ADHD. Neither does being unable to sit through a whole movie anymore without doing something else. That’s because it’s just easier to get distracted when there’s less structure in your life. The pandemic gave us more unstructured time for our minds to flit to the next thing before we really even got into the first. To change that, it’s important to create structure where you can: Use a planner, avoid email on the weekends, or designate specific times to do your foam rolling or have virtual coffee with your brother. These bricks of certainty can go a long way to keeping you attentive.

Still, you might find yourself checking all the boxes on an online ADHD assessment as Cooper did—ticking off yes to having difficulty concentrating on what people say to you, finding it hard to relax when you have time to yourself, and putting things off till the last minute. But if your problems are new for you, it’s probably not ADHD.

It’s crucial to tease out ADHD from other mental illnesses, because almost all of them—including depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder—can impair attention like ADHD. So can feeling tired or bored. You need to figure out what’s going on so you can get the proper treatment.

Among the first things I do when someone is concerned about ADHD is ask when they started losing their focus and whether they have trouble at work, at home, or both. Since ADHD is a condition that affects the brain, it doesn’t pick and choose where it’s going to turn on and off. If you can’t focus at work, you’re probably not able to focus at home, either.

In Cooper’s case, I learned that he had done well in school all the way up through his master’s degree and never really had difficulty staying on point until recently. That’s when he started losing his keys, too. But there were also a lot of changes in his life: Work was exceptionally busy, he was saving for a house, and he had just broken up with his longtime girlfriend. And, of course, there was a pandemic. He admitted to feeling burned out and to having a hard time motivating himself to do almost anything. He’d felt that way for weeks, and he was so keyed up that he wasn’t sleeping that well, either.

These were all signs of depression, a condition that even in mild forms can make it almost impossible to focus. So while the symptoms might look like ADHD, depression requires an entirely different approach to treatment.

Get the right treatment and bring focus back

Don’t get me wrong; I understand why patients ask for stimulants like Ritalin and Adderall—they are a first-line treatment for ADHD. But they are also controlled meds that can worsen anxiety or precipitate mania or psychosis if you have bipolar disorder. Even if you don’t have a mental-health condition, stimulants can have side effects like interfering with your sleep, speeding up your heart rate, or causing you to lose your appetite. So there’s lots to consider before deciding to take one.

Depending on what’s going on, people with focus issues may get medications for depression or anxiety that can help put them in a better headspace to process what’s happening. Mindfulness-based practices, such as yoga, can also help slow racing thoughts and invite a sense of stillness. Another option is cognitive behavioral therapy, which can help you explore irrational thought patterns that are throwing you off.

Since Cooper was experiencing mild depression, he and I began weekly talk-therapy sessions, and I wrote a prescription for Wellbutrin, an antidepressant that can also help boost concentration. He learned to use mindfulness techniques to help slow his thoughts. Once Cooper had a chance to process all of the changes in his life, his mood, motivation, and attention improved within a few weeks, and so did his ability to get his work done.

Bottom line: Nothing works better than a stimulant for treating ADHD, but most of the suspected ADHD cases that walk through my door aren’t ADHD at all. That doesn’t make them less important to treat. If you just can’t focus on anything anymore, it’s worth investing time and energy to get to the bottom of it. There are solutions that can help you get back to being yourself.
