Improving IVF Success: Can PRP Help with Embryo Implantation?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy is an emerging IVF treatment that may improve endometrial thickness and implantation rates in patients with a thin uterine lining. Clinical studies show PRP can increase endometrial thickness by 1 to 3 mm and boost implantation rates to 25–30%, compared to just 10–15% without PRP.

Because PRP uses your own blood, it is generally considered safe. However, it remains experimental and carries potential risks, including infection, mild cramping, and unknown long-term effects. Key factors to weigh before proceeding include patient candidacy, PRP preparation protocols, treatment timing, and cost.

Explanation

In vitro fertilization (IVF) offers hope to many couples struggling with infertility. A key step in this process is embryo implantation, where a healthy embryo attaches to the uterine lining (endometrium). Some patients, however, face challenges when their uterine lining is too thin, reducing the chances of successful implantation. Recently, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has emerged as a potential therapy for improving endometrial thickness and receptivity. This article explores the evidence, benefits, risks, and practical considerations of using PRP for thin uterine lining in IVF cycles.

What Is PRP and How Might It Work?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a concentration of platelets derived from a patient's own blood. Platelets contain growth factors and cytokines that promote tissue repair and regeneration.

Key components of PRP:

Platelets: Small blood cells that release growth factors.

Growth Factors: Proteins like PDGF, VEGF, and TGF-β that stimulate cell proliferation and blood vessel formation.

Plasma: The fluid portion of blood that carries platelets and proteins.

Proposed actions in the uterus:

Stimulate endometrial cell growth

Enhance blood flow to the lining

Promote vascularization (formation of new blood vessels)

Why a Thin Uterine Lining Matters

A healthy endometrial lining typically measures between 7 mm and 14 mm on ultrasound at the time of embryo transfer. When the lining is under 7 mm, studies suggest lower implantation and pregnancy rates.

Reasons for thin lining include:

Previous uterine surgeries (e.g., curettage or fibroid removal)

Chronic uterine inflammation (endometritis)

Hormonal imbalances

Poor blood flow to the uterus

Evidence for PRP in Reproductive Medicine

Several small clinical studies and case reports have explored PRP for thin uterine lining:

Endometrial Thickness Improvement: Patients receiving intrauterine PRP infusions showed increases of 1–3 mm in lining thickness over one or two treatment cycles.

Implantation and Pregnancy Rates: Preliminary data suggest a higher implantation rate (up to 25–30%) in PRP-treated cycles, compared to 10–15% in similar patients without PRP.

Live Births Reported: A handful of case series reported successful live births following PRP-augmented IVF cycles in women with previously thin linings and repeated implantation failures.

Limitations of current research:

Small sample sizes (often under 50 participants)

Lack of large, randomized controlled trials

Variability in PRP preparation methods and treatment protocols

Possible Risks and Considerations

While PRP is generally considered safe, it's important to discuss potential downsides:

Infection Risk: Though rare, any intrauterine procedure carries a small risk of infection.

Discomfort or Spotting: Mild cramping or light bleeding may occur after infusion.

Unknown Long-Term Effects: Limited data exist on the long-term safety of intrauterine PRP.

Cost and Accessibility: PRP preparation requires specialized equipment and trained personnel; costs vary widely and may not be covered by insurance.

How PRP Treatment Is Typically Done

Blood Collection: 20–60 mL of your blood is drawn, usually from a vein in the arm.

Centrifugation: The blood is spun in a centrifuge to separate red blood cells from plasma and platelets.

PRP Preparation: The platelet-rich layer is isolated, sometimes activated with calcium or thrombin.

Intrauterine Infusion: A thin catheter is used to gently infuse PRP into the uterine cavity.

Monitoring: Ultrasound checks are performed to measure lining thickness before and after PRP.

Clinics vary in timing—some administer PRP during the estrogen phase of the cycle, others right before embryo transfer. Discuss protocol specifics with your fertility specialist.

Who Might Be a Good Candidate?

PRP for thin uterine lining may be considered if you:

Have an endometrial thickness consistently under 7 mm in previous IVF cycles.

Experienced repeated implantation failure (two or more failed transfers).

Have ruled out other causes of thin lining (hormonal issues, chronic inflammation).

Are willing to try an experimental therapy with limited but promising data.
